#+STARTUP: overview

* Introduction

This document explains how to build a self-contained web-based media literacy
resource of the kind used in the "Source Audit" classroom activity. The
approach involves building a set of realistic-looking HTML pages that simulate
different kinds of online sources — from peer-reviewed journals to wellness
blogs to native advertising — and using them as the basis for a structured
evaluation activity with students.

The full project (at the time of writing) covers five topics in Year 9 Science:
Vaccines, Antibiotics & Resistance, Semmelweis & Handwashing, Joseph Lister &
Sterilisation, and How Clinical Trials Work. Each topic has five curated
"source" pages, and the site is hosted on GitHub Pages. The whole thing is
static HTML with no build system, no framework, and no back end.

This guide covers:
- The pedagogical rationale for the design
- The spectrum of source quality types and how to think about them
- Detailed instructions (with HTML/CSS snippets) for building each source type
- The "invisible scaffold" technique — the HUD overlay
- The classroom activity design and printable materials
- How to adapt this for other subjects

** What makes this approach work

The core insight is that students learn to evaluate sources best when they have
to work out the difference for themselves, rather than being told. If you show
students a trustworthy source and a bad source side by side and explain why
one is better, they learn the conclusion. If they have to investigate five
sources — each designed to be a realistic example of its type — and rank them
without being told what they're looking for, they discover the signals
independently.

This is only possible if the sources are good enough to be convincing. A
fake "bad source" that looks obviously fake does not teach the lesson. A fake
wellness blog that looks and reads like an actual wellness blog — including the
product callouts, the "as seen in" credibility badges, the emotionally
warm tone, the affiliate notice in tiny text — teaches students something
real about how such sources operate online.

The design goal is: every source should be believable in its genre. A student
should be able to encounter any one of them in the wild and not immediately
recognise it as constructed.


* The Spectrum of Information Quality

The project uses seven source types, ordered here from most to least reliable
for evidence-based factual claims.

** 1. Gold Standard

What it is: A source with named expert authors, institutional affiliations,
peer-review credentials (real or simulated), specific numbered citations,
and a neutral, methodical tone. In the real world this includes academic
journal articles, institutional review publications, WHO/government reports,
and encyclopaedia-style reference articles with verified facts.

Tells that make it trustworthy:
- Named authors with affiliations (university, hospital, research institute)
- Received/Accepted/Published dates showing an editorial process
- A DOI or ISSN
- Numbered inline citations matched to a reference list
- Abstract or structured summary
- Data tables with sourced figures
- No product placement, no ads, no commercial interest

** 2. Quality Journalism

What it is: Original reporting by a named journalist, drawing on primary
sources (interviews, OIA requests, government data, named experts). The
journalist has a byline, credentials, and a clearly editorial-independent
masthead. No product to sell. May include a corrections policy.

Tells that make it trustworthy:
- Named journalist with a track record
- Specific named sources (not "a researcher", but "Dr X, University of Y")
- Primary data (OIA documents, official statistics)
- Transparent methodology
- No advertising content adjacent to the article
- Masthead with editorial independence statement

** 3. Churnalism

What it is: Content that looks like journalism but is mostly repackaged
information — often a wire story or press release processed into an article
with minimal original reporting. Characterised by vague sourcing ("researchers
say", "according to a new study"), clickbait headlines, high ad density, and
no original investigation.

Tells for students:
- "According to researchers at a European university" — no name, no study title
- Headline makes a dramatic claim; article walks it back
- "We reached out to X and did not receive a response before publication"
- View/share counts displayed prominently
- Article full of ads, recommended-content widgets, share buttons
- Author credit says "Staff" or just a first name

** 4. Biased / Partisan / Personal Blog

What it is: A personal perspective presented as health information. May
contain accurate facts but the framing, selection of evidence, and emotional
language serve an agenda (often anti-establishment, anti-pharma, or
pro-alternative-medicine). The author's credentials are either absent or
not relevant to the claims being made.

Tells for students:
- Warm, personal, emotionally engaging tone — "As a parent, I..."
- Rhetorical questions as section headings: "But what are they not telling us?"
- Loaded language: "the establishment", "suppressed", "what they don't want
  you to know"
- Author credentials are lifestyle-based, not scientific
- Missing counter-evidence: the science is cherry-picked
- No specific citations, or citations that don't support the claim made

** 5. Native Advertising

What it is: Content that looks editorial but is produced to sell something.
A brand pays for an article that reads like health journalism, builds trust
with accurate information, and then transitions to recommending the brand's
products. The "Sponsored Content" or "Advertising" label is technically
present but designed to be missed.

Tells for students:
- "Sponsored Content" label is tiny, grayed out, and easy to miss
- Article structure: accurate information → concern → our product solves this
- Product callout boxes mid-article with star ratings and "Add to Cart"
- Author is listed as "Brand Ambassador" or "our team"
- Sidebar full of products from the same company
- Footer disclaimer: "This product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure,
  or prevent any disease"

** 6. Misinformation (honest mistake)

What it is: Content produced with good intent that contains factual errors —
usually because it is outdated, because the author misunderstood the source
material, or because it was written without expert review. The key distinction
from disinformation: there is no intent to deceive.

In this project the main example is a "teacher-created classroom resource" last
updated in 2007 that contains several factual errors about Semmelweis —
including the wrong year of his death, and the claim that doctors immediately
accepted his findings. It looks credible because it looks like a school
resource, but it has not been maintained.

Tells for students:
- Last updated date is very old
- No author credentials visible
- Layout looks educational/trustworthy but the content doesn't match other
  sources on the same facts
- Inconsistencies when cross-checked against a known-good source

** 7. Disinformation (or disinformation-adjacent)

What it is: Content that uses real, accurate facts as a foundation and then
builds to a conclusion that the evidence does not support — often a sweeping
conspiratorial claim. The author may believe what they are writing, but the
rhetorical structure is designed to make the reader reject a whole category
of evidence (e.g. "all clinical trials are manipulated", "the establishment
silences anyone who challenges them").

Tells for students:
- Opens with something accurate and uncontroversial
- Uses real examples of genuine failure/scandal to generalise to "all of X
  is corrupt"
- Language becomes increasingly loaded: "they", "suppressed", "silenced"
- No distinguishing between "some trials were fraudulent" and "all trials
  are fraudulent"
- Uses the reader's justified concern to push them toward wholesale rejection

The distinction between this and a personal blog is important: a biased blog
makes a specific claim with bad evidence. Disinformation-adjacent content
systematically undermines the reader's ability to evaluate any future evidence.


* How to Build Each Source Type

The following sections give practical HTML/CSS design guidance for each type,
with annotated snippets from the actual project files.

** General structure of every source page

Every source page in this project has two CSS blocks in its <head>:

1. The page-specific styles (which vary per source type)
2. The HUD (the teacher overlay — identical across all pages)

The HUD is discussed in detail in section 4. Every page should follow this
template:

#+BEGIN_SRC html
<!DOCTYPE html>
<html lang="en">
<head>
  <meta charset="UTF-8">
  <meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1.0">
  <title>[Title that matches the fake source's style]</title>
  <!-- Source-type-specific font and styles -->
  <style>
    /* ... page styles ... */
  </style>
  <!-- HUD overlay — identical in all pages -->
  <style>
    .hud-top-bar { position:fixed; top:0; left:0; width:100%; height:60px;
      background:#1a2744; border-bottom:4px solid #0d7a6e; display:flex;
      align-items:center; z-index:9999; }
    /* ... full HUD CSS ... */
    body { padding-top:70px !important; }
  </style>
</head>
<body>
<div class="hud-top-bar">...</div>
<!-- source page content below -->
</body>
</html>
#+END_SRC

** Building a Gold Standard source (academic journal)

The goal is to simulate a peer-reviewed journal article. Students with even
a little research experience will recognise the genre immediately. Key design
elements:

*Visual identity:*
- Serif body font (Lora, Source Serif 4, or Georgia) — academic publications
  use serif type for body text to signal scholarly register
- Maroon/navy colour scheme — journals rarely use bright colours
- Two-column layout: article left, sidebar right
- Article metadata block at the top (not a bio box, a structured metadata table)

*The metadata block — this is the most important element:*

#+BEGIN_SRC html
<div class="article-meta-block">
  <div class="meta-row">
    <span class="meta-label">Authors</span>
    <span class="meta-value">
      Dr. Hemi Ngata<sup>1</sup>, Dr. Claire Sutherland<sup>2</sup>
    </span>
  </div>
  <div class="meta-row">
    <span class="meta-label">Affiliations</span>
    <span class="meta-value">
      <sup>1</sup>School of Biological Sciences, Victoria University of Wellington;
      <sup>2</sup>Institute for Environmental Science and Research (ESR), Porirua
    </span>
  </div>
  <div class="meta-row">
    <span class="meta-label">Received</span>
    <span class="meta-value">4 October 2023</span>
  </div>
  <div class="meta-row">
    <span class="meta-label">Accepted</span>
    <span class="meta-value">19 January 2024 (following peer review)</span>
  </div>
  <div class="meta-row">
    <span class="meta-label">DOI</span>
    <span class="meta-value">10.1186/jame.2024.0312</span>
  </div>
</div>
#+END_SRC

The Received/Accepted dates demonstrate a peer review process. The DOI is
fictitious but follows real DOI format. The university affiliations are real
NZ institutions, which is important — if students Google them, they exist.
The author names are plausible but not real people.

*The abstract box:*

#+BEGIN_SRC html
<div class="abstract-box">
  <h3>Abstract</h3>
  <p>Antibiotics have been among the most transformative medicines in human
  history... [continues with accurate, technical content]</p>
</div>
#+END_SRC

*Inline citations — essential for the gold standard feel:*

#+BEGIN_SRC html
<p>The WHO's 2023 Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance System
report estimated that bacterial AMR was directly responsible for 1.27 million
deaths globally in 2019<sup class="ref">[10]</sup>.</p>
#+END_SRC

Every factual claim should have a superscript reference number. The reference
list at the bottom should use a real citation format (APA, Vancouver, or AMA
are all used in life sciences). The references should be real, checkable
publications — this is important. If a student googles "Waksman 1947 antibiotic
Mycologia", the paper should exist.

*Data table — makes the source feel substantive:*

#+BEGIN_SRC html
<table class="data-table">
  <thead>
    <tr>
      <th>Mechanism</th>
      <th>Effect on Bacteria</th>
      <th>Antibiotic Class Example</th>
    </tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr>
      <td>Cell wall synthesis inhibition</td>
      <td>Bacteria cannot maintain their cell wall; they swell and burst</td>
      <td>Penicillins, cephalosporins, vancomycin</td>
    </tr>
    <!-- ... -->
  </tbody>
</table>
#+END_SRC

*Sidebar — article info, not products:*

#+BEGIN_SRC html
<div class="sidebar-box">
  <h4>Article Info</h4>
  <p><strong>Peer reviewed:</strong> Yes (double-blind)</p>
  <p><strong>Open access:</strong> Yes</p>
  <p><strong>Licence:</strong> CC BY 4.0</p>
</div>
<div class="sidebar-box">
  <h4>External Links</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><a href="https://www.who.int/...">WHO: AMR Fact Sheet</a></li>
    <li><a href="https://www.cdc.gov/...">CDC: Antibiotic Resistance</a></li>
  </ul>
</div>
#+END_SRC

Note: the external links in the sidebar should point to real URLs. The WHO
and CDC pages linked in the project are real. This is intentional — if a
student clicks through, they reach actual authoritative sources.

*Footer:*

#+BEGIN_SRC html
<footer>
  &copy; 2024 Journal of Applied Microbiology Education.
  Open access under CC BY 4.0. For educational use.
  Content does not substitute professional medical advice.
</footer>
#+END_SRC

The journal name, ISSN, and volume/issue information are all fictitious but
formatted correctly.

** Building a Quality Journalism source (investigative/data journalism)

The goal is to simulate long-form investigative journalism of the kind
published by outlets like Stuff, The Spinoff, RNZ, or The Guardian. The key
signals are: a named journalist with stated credentials, named and quoted
expert sources, primary data (in the NZ context, often OIA documents), and
a clean, ad-free layout.

*Visual identity:*
- Clean, editorial masthead with a distinctive wordmark
- Serif display font for headlines (Source Serif 4, Georgia)
- Red accent colour (traditional newspaper association)
- No ads — this is one of the strongest signals of quality journalism
- A byline with journalist credentials

*The byline — the most critical element:*

#+BEGIN_SRC html
<div class="article-byline">
  <div class="byline-left">
    <div class="byline-author">Aroha Tūhoe</div>
    <div class="byline-credentials">
      Science Correspondent · NZ Health Investigates
    </div>
    <div class="byline-meta">
      <span>Published 14 February 2025</span>
      <span>Updated 16 February 2025</span>
      <span>8 min read</span>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>
#+END_SRC

The journalist has a full name, a job title, and a publication. The article
has both a publication date and an update date (indicating ongoing editorial
attention).

*Named expert sources in-text:*

#+BEGIN_SRC html
<div class="box-expert">
  <div class="expert-name">Associate Professor James Liu</div>
  <div class="expert-role">
    Institute of Environmental Science and Research, Massey University
  </div>
  <p>"These numbers should concern us. The pattern we're seeing in farm
  antibiotic use is exactly what the WHO has been warning about for years."</p>
</div>
#+END_SRC

The expert box gives full name, affiliation, and a direct quote — not a
paraphrase. Sources are named, not described vaguely.

*Data callout — showing primary data:*

#+BEGIN_SRC html
<div class="data-callout">
  <div class="data-label">OIA-Released Government Data</div>
  <div class="data-number">+34%</div>
  <div class="data-desc">
    Increase in antibiotic use on NZ farms 2019–2024,
    measured in milligrams per population correction unit (PCU)
  </div>
  <div class="data-source">
    Source: Ministry for Primary Industries, released under OIA request
    #2024-0892, 18 November 2024
  </div>
</div>
#+END_SRC

The specific OIA reference number, date, and ministry name give this the
feel of genuine document-based reporting. Students who know what an OIA is
will immediately recognise the signal.

*Author bio and corrections policy — signals of editorial accountability:*

#+BEGIN_SRC html
<div class="author-bio">
  <div class="author-bio-name">Aroha Tūhoe</div>
  <div class="author-bio-title">Science Correspondent</div>
  <div class="author-bio-text">
    Aroha Tūhoe covers health policy and environmental science for NZ Health
    Investigates. She holds a BSc (Microbiology) from the University of Otago
    and an MJ (Journalism) from AUT.
  </div>
</div>

<div class="editorial-footer">
  <p><strong>Corrections:</strong> NZ Health Investigates is committed to
  accuracy. If you believe this article contains an error, please
  <a href="#">contact our editorial team</a>.</p>
</div>
#+END_SRC

The corrections policy is a strong signal of quality journalism. No
commercial content outlet or personal blog will have one.

** Building a Churnalism source (health news aggregator)

The goal is to simulate the kind of health news that gets shared widely on
social media — articles with dramatic headlines, thin reporting, vague
sourcing, and heavy monetisation. Think clickbait health news aggregators.

*Visual identity:*
- Bold, aggressive sans-serif fonts (Open Sans, Roboto at heavy weights)
- Orange or red accent colours — high-energy, urgent palette
- "Breaking News" bar at the top
- Share count prominently displayed
- Multiple ad placeholders visible on the page
- "You Might Also Like" widget with more clickbait headlines in the sidebar

*The headline and standfirst — dramatic claim, vague body:*

#+BEGIN_SRC html
<h1 class="article-headline">
  Scientists Say Vaccine Immunity May Be Fading Faster Than We Thought
  — Here's What That Means
</h1>
<p class="article-standfirst">
  A new study is raising questions about how long vaccine protection
  actually lasts — and some experts say the findings could change the
  way we think about booster programmes entirely.
</p>
#+END_SRC

Note the headline makes a strong claim. The standfirst softens it slightly
("raising questions"). The article body never identifies the study, the
researchers, or where it was published.

*The author byline — no individual named:*

#+BEGIN_SRC html
<div class="article-meta">
  <span class="freshness">Published 2 hours ago</span>
  <span class="author">HealthBuzz Staff</span>
  <span>3 min read</span>
  <span>🔥 14,302 views</span>
</div>
#+END_SRC

"HealthBuzz Staff" — no individual named. The freshness indicator ("2 hours
ago") and the view count both signal content designed for rapid consumption
and sharing, not careful reading.

*The vague sourcing — the core of churnalism:*

#+BEGIN_SRC html
<p>According to researchers at a European university — results that have
not yet been peer reviewed but were posted to a preprint server this week
— the rate at which neutralising antibodies decline post-vaccination is
steeper than the models assumed.</p>

<p>A new study published in a major international journal found that
participants who received two doses showed a significant drop in measurable
antibodies by the six-month mark.</p>

<p>"Antibody levels are just one piece of the picture," one researcher
told a science newswire.</p>
#+END_SRC

"A European university" — which one? "A major international journal" —
which journal? "One researcher told a science newswire" — which researcher?
This is precisely the vague sourcing pattern that distinguishes churnalism
from journalism. Every claim has a source gesture but no actual source.

*Ad placeholders — heavy monetisation:*

#+BEGIN_SRC html
<div class="in-article-ad">
  <div class="ad-label">Advertisement</div>
  <div class="ad-placeholder rectangle">[ RECTANGLE AD — 300×250 ]</div>
</div>
#+END_SRC

Multiple ad placeholders appear in the article body and sidebar. In a real
churnalism site these would be Google AdSense or similar. In the simulated
version they're placeholders, but the structure and density is accurate.

*The "You Might Also Like" sidebar — designed to keep you clicking:*

#+BEGIN_SRC html
<div class="you-might-like">
  <a href="#">
    <div class="yml-thumb">IMG</div>
    <div class="yml-text">
      This Common Household Product May Be Destroying Your Immune System
      <div class="yml-meta">4.2k shares · 1 hr ago</div>
    </div>
  </a>
  <a href="#">
    <!-- more clickbait headlines -->
  </a>
</div>
#+END_SRC

The headlines in the "You Might Also Like" sidebar are deliberately written
in churnalism style: alarming, vague, share-optimised. This is an important
detail — the content around an article is part of the source evaluation.

** Building a Native Advertising source

This is the most sophisticated source type to build, because it must look
genuinely editorial while being structurally commercial. There are two
subtypes demonstrated in the project:

*Subtype A: Corporate brand blog (PureLife Wellness NZ)*

A brand publishes content in the style of health journalism. The content is
factually accurate (this is crucial — the native ad only works if people
trust it). The commercial intent is revealed only via a tiny "Sponsored
Content" label and the product callouts embedded mid-article.

*Sponsored notice — technically present but easy to miss:*

#+BEGIN_SRC html
<div class="sponsored-notice">
  <strong>Sponsored Content</strong>
  · This article is brought to you by PureLife Wellness NZ
</div>
#+END_SRC

The CSS for this element:

#+BEGIN_SRC css
.sponsored-notice {
  background: #f5f0e8;
  border-bottom: 1px solid #ddd8ce;
  padding: 7px 0;
  text-align: center;
  font-size: 0.7rem;      /* very small */
  color: #888;            /* low contrast grey */
  letter-spacing: 0.5px;
}
.sponsored-notice strong {
  color: #666;            /* still low contrast */
}
#+END_SRC

The key design choice: 0.7rem font size, #888 grey text on a beige background.
This is technically disclosure. It is designed to be ignored. Real native
advertising works exactly this way.

*The article structure — trust-building before the sell:*

The article follows a three-act structure that is standard in native
advertising:

Act 1: Build credibility with accurate, helpful information.
The article accurately explains vaccine ingredients — aluminium adjuvants,
formaldehyde, thimerosal — in a way that is factually correct but framed
to seem alarming. This is real information, and it builds trust.

Act 2: Raise concern without making false claims.
"Some researchers have called for more long-term studies on cumulative exposure."
This is technically accurate but selected to create doubt.

Act 3: Introduce the product as the natural solution.
After establishing immune support as important, the product appears:

#+BEGIN_SRC html
<div class="product-callout">
  <div class="product-img">
    <div class="product-icon">🍓</div>
    ImmunoShield Junior<br>Berry Flavour
  </div>
  <div class="product-info">
    <div class="product-name">ImmunoShield Junior™</div>
    <div class="product-tagline">
      Complete daily immune support for children aged 3–12
    </div>
    <div class="product-stars">
      ★★★★★ <span>(247 reviews)</span>
    </div>
    <div class="product-price">$38.90 <span>/ 60 chewable tablets</span></div>
    <a href="#" class="product-btn">Add to Cart</a>
    <div class="disclaimer-tiny">
      This product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure,
      or prevent any disease. This is not medical advice.
    </div>
  </div>
</div>
#+END_SRC

The product callout appears mid-article, between informational sections.
The tiny disclaimer is legally required and practically unread. Two product
callouts appear in the article body; more products appear in the sidebar.

*Subtype B: Personal brand ambassador blog (GutWise NZ)*

Different from the corporate blog: this is written in first person by a named
individual ("Sophie M.") who is labelled a "Brand Ambassador" — but this label
appears in very small text.

#+BEGIN_SRC html
<div class="author-bar">
  <div class="author-photo">😊</div>
  <div class="author-info">
    <div class="author-name">Sophie M.</div>
    <div class="author-role">GutWise Brand Ambassador · Certified Nutrition Coach</div>
    <div class="post-meta">Published March 2024 · 9 min read · Wellington, NZ</div>
  </div>
</div>
#+END_SRC

"Brand Ambassador" is disclosed but presented as equivalent to a credential.
The personal, first-person narrative structure ("I was about six weeks
post-antibiotics when...") creates emotional connection and trust that a
corporate-voice article cannot.

The article also contains an affiliate notice — technically present, barely
visible:

#+BEGIN_SRC html
<div class="affiliate-notice">
  This post contains affiliate links. If you purchase through our links,
  we may earn a commission — at no extra cost to you.
  <a href="#">What does this mean?</a>
</div>
#+END_SRC

CSS:

#+BEGIN_SRC css
.affiliate-notice {
  background: #f0faf5;
  border-bottom: 1px solid #d0f0e0;
  text-align: center;
  padding: 6px;
  font-size: 0.68rem;   /* smaller than the sponsored notice */
  color: #888;
}
#+END_SRC

0.68rem. Even smaller than the sponsored content notice. This is accurate
to real affiliate-blog practice.

** Building a Biased / Personal Blog source

The goal is to simulate a wellness parent blog — emotionally warm, personally
grounded, genuinely caring in tone, but factually selective and operating from
an anti-establishment worldview. The challenge is that this type of source can
be quite accurate on individual facts; the problem is with the framing and
what is left out.

*Visual identity:*
- Warm, soft colour palette (sage green, blush, ivory)
- Elegant serif display font (Cormorant Garamond is perfect — it reads as
  refined and authentic, not corporate)
- Personal handwritten-style elements in the header
- No hard news elements — no date stamps in orange, no share counts
- "About Me" box in the sidebar with a hand-drawn avatar style

#+BEGIN_SRC css
:root {
  --sage: #7a9a6a;
  --sage-dark: #5a7a4a;
  --blush: #e8c4b0;
  --blush-light: #fdf6f2;
  --warm: #8a6a4a;
  --bg: #fdfaf6;
}
body {
  font-family: 'Lato', sans-serif;
  background: var(--bg);
  color: var(--text);
}
.blog-title {
  font-family: 'Cormorant Garamond', serif;
  font-size: 2.5rem;
  font-weight: 300;  /* light weight — graceful, personal */
  color: var(--sage-dark);
}
#+END_SRC

*The author — wellness credentials, not scientific ones:*

#+BEGIN_SRC html
<div class="author-tag">
  <div class="author-circle">K</div>
  <div class="info">
    <div class="name">Katrina Wells</div>
    <div class="role">
      Holistic nutritionist, mum of three, natural health advocate
    </div>
  </div>
</div>
#+END_SRC

"Holistic nutritionist" and "natural health advocate" are not regulated
credentials in NZ. This is a real and important point for students: anyone can
call themselves a nutritionist; only a Registered Dietitian (RD) has a
protected title backed by university training and professional regulation.

*The personal opening — emotional engagement before argument:*

The article should open with a relatable personal story that establishes
empathy before making any claims. "I was that mum who followed every piece
of advice my GP gave without question..." This is a deliberate rhetorical
technique that builds identification with the reader before asking them to
doubt official information.

*Rhetorical questions as section headings:*

#+BEGIN_SRC html
<h2>But Are We Being Told the Whole Story?</h2>
<h2>Why Does This Matter for Our Children?</h2>
#+END_SRC

Framing questions as headings creates the impression of investigation without
requiring evidence.

*The personal block — stylised pull quote from self:*

#+BEGIN_SRC html
<div class="personal-block">
  "I am not anti-vaccine. I want to be really clear about that.
   What I am is pro-information — and I think those are very different things."
</div>
#+END_SRC

This is a classic structure in wellness-blog anti-establishment content:
the preemptive denial ("I am not anti-X") followed by behaviour that is
functionally anti-X. It is designed to neutralise the most obvious criticism
before a reader can make it.

** Building a Misinformation (honest mistake) source

The goal is to simulate a genuine, well-intentioned educational resource that
contains factual errors — either because it is outdated or because the author
was working outside their expertise. The Semmelweis classroom resource is
the primary example.

*Visual identity — this is the key challenge:*

The source must look trustworthy. It should look like something a teacher
would have bookmarked in 2007 and forgotten to update. This means:
- Old-school web design (no Google Fonts — system fonts only)
- Blue/yellow government-style colour scheme
- Fixed-width centred layout (pre-responsive design)
- Actual visible signs of age: "Last updated: September 2007"

*The factual errors — deliberate but not immediately obvious:*

In this project the Semmelweis classroom resource contains three errors:
1. Semmelweis died in 1868 (the real date is 1865)
2. It credits him with "discovering germ theory" (he preceded formal germ
   theory and his explanation was pre-Pasteur)
3. It claims doctors immediately accepted his handwashing findings (in reality
   he was widely resisted for years)

These errors are not invented carelessly — they are based on real
misconceptions that circulate widely. Students who compare this source against
the gold standard source should notice the discrepancies in the death year
(the most checkable fact) and the claim about immediate acceptance
(which contradicts the whole Semmelweis narrative).

A table of contents signals organisation and care — it looks like work was

** Building a Disinformation (or disinformation-adjacent) source

The goal is to simulate content that uses genuine scandals as leverage for
sweeping, unfalsifiable claims about entire institutions. The Clinical Trials
"Big Pharma" source is the primary example.

*Visual identity:*
- Dark navy/black colour scheme — authoritative, conspiratorial
- Gold accents — "truth" signalling
- The site name contains words like "Independent", "Observer", "Truth",
  "Investigates"
- "Mission statement" sidebar explaining why this site exists to fight
  against "captured media"

</div>

The conclusion box moves from "here are real documented cases" to "this is
not the exception, it is the pattern" to "all of this is marketing". This
is the logical leap that distinguishes the disinformation structure: real
examples used to support a claim that no evidence could falsify.


* The "Invisible Scaffold" — The HUD Overlay

The single most important technical element in the project is the HUD
(heads-up display) — a fixed navigation bar overlaid on every source page
that anchors it within the classroom context without disrupting the reading
experience.

** What the HUD does

Every source page looks like an external website. Without the HUD, students
would lose context — they might forget which topic they are supposed to be
looking at, or how the source they are reading connects to the Hub.

The HUD provides:
1. A breadcrumb back to the Hub (Home → Topic → Source)
2. A badge reminding them they are doing "External Source Analysis"
3. A persistent visual anchor that all pages belong to the same system

The HUD is designed to look like a browser extension or tool overlay — not
part of the fake website's own design.

** The topic hub page

The topic hub page lists the five sources for a topic. The design is
deliberately neutral — no quality indicators, no colour coding, all sources
look identical. This is intentional: if the hub page hinted at quality
(green for good, red for bad), the exercise would be compromised.

The source order on each hub page is shuffled so the gold standard source
does not always appear at position 1. In this project:
- Vaccines: Gold standard at S2
- Antibiotics: Gold standard at S3
- Semmelweis: Gold standard at S4
- Lister: Gold standard at S4
- Clinical Trials: Gold standard at S5

This ensures students cannot use source number as a proxy for quality.

2. Push all files to the main branch with the root at /
3. In the repository settings, enable GitHub Pages from the main branch root
4. The site will be live at https://[username].github.io/[repo-name]

This gives you a shareable URL for classroom use. Students can access it on
any device without installing anything.


* The Classroom Activity Design

** Overview

The activity is a jigsaw structure — each student becomes an expert on one
source, then shares findings with a group that covers all five sources.

Students work in 5 groups (one per topic). Each person reads one source,
fills in an evaluation card independently, and then the group shares findings
and ranks all five sources from most to least trustworthy.

A class debrief then surfaces patterns across all groups, and the teacher
reveals the source type names at the end.

** Why the jigsaw structure works

The jigsaw is more effective than giving every student all five sources
because:
1. Each student reads one source thoroughly rather than skimming five
2. Students must explain their source to others — explanation reinforces
   understanding
3. Groups must debate and justify rankings — the disagreement is productive
4. Each student gets a card with their source's title, so they cannot be
   confused about which source they are evaluating

** The student evaluation card

Each student receives a physical card (printed landscape A4, 2 cards per sheet,
25 total) with:
- The topic group name and colour (teal for Vaccines, navy for Antibiotics, etc.)
- The source number and title
- Five evaluation questions with checkbox options
- A notes line after each question
- A rating scale from 1 (don't trust) to 5 (fully trust)
- A "main reason for my rating" line

The five questions are:
1. Who is behind this? (named expert / organisation / anonymous)
2. What do they want from you? (inform / persuade / sell / hard to tell)
3. How is evidence used? (named studies / vague references / personal stories)
4. What's missing? (other side / credentials / date)
5. What's the tone? (calm and balanced / alarming / very confident)

These questions are deliberately framework-light. They do not use the
technical vocabulary (churnalism, native advertising, etc.) because the
goal is for students to discover those patterns themselves. The questions
function as structured observation prompts, not a classification rubric.

** The group ranking sheet

Each group of 5 students receives a shared group sheet. They must:
1. Fill in the source titles and individual ratings in a ranking table
2. Rank all five sources 1 to 5 (no ties allowed)
3. Answer three synthesis questions:
   - What did your least trustworthy source do to seem credible?
   - What was the biggest difference between your #1 and #5 source?
   - Did any source surprise you?

The "no ties" rule forces students to make a judgment call when two sources
feel similarly problematic. This productive difficulty is where much of the
learning happens.

** The teacher guide

The teacher guide (print-only, not shared with students) contains:

1. A timing reference (0–3 min setup, 3–18 min individual, 18–28 min group,
   28–38 min debrief, 38–40 min exit)

2. A source answer key — one row per student card — showing the source type
   and the key red flag to draw out in the debrief

3. A debrief sequence — five questions in order, with expected student
   responses

4. Notes on the "trap" sources (the Semmelweis 2007 classroom resource;
   the native advertising sources that look editorial)

The critical rule communicated in the teacher guide:
Do not tell students the sources vary in quality before the activity.
The debrief is the reveal moment. If students are told in advance that they
are looking for "bad" sources, they will approach the exercise as a sorting
game rather than as genuine evaluation.

** Running with 20 students instead of 25

With exactly 5 groups of 4: skip Source 5 from each group's ranking, or
assign one student two sources (they skim the second after finishing the first).
The ranking and debrief work fine with 4 sources per group.


* Adapting for Other Subjects

This approach is not specific to science or medicine. Any subject area with
a range of publicly available source types can use this structure.

** Adapting the topics

For humanities or social studies, good topic areas include:
- Any contested historical event (sources range from academic history to
  national myth to political revision)
- Climate change (scientific consensus / quality journalism / denial /
  commercial greenwashing)
- Social media and mental health (peer-reviewed studies / popular psychology /
  wellness influencers / tech company PR)

For business or economics:
- A major corporate event (annual reports / investigative journalism /
  PR / conspiracy)

The principle is the same: choose a topic where a full spectrum of source
types naturally exists.

** Adapting the source types

Not all subject areas will have a "misinformation teacher resource" that
works as well as the Semmelweis example. The most universally applicable
types are:
- Gold standard (always possible — academic paper, official report)
- Churnalism (always possible — any topic generates content farming)
- Native advertising (always possible — any topic has something to sell)
- Biased/personal blog (always possible — any topic has advocates)

Quality journalism and disinformation are more topic-dependent. Not every
area will have investigative journalism with OIA data, and disinformation
tends to cluster around topics where there is strong emotional investment
(health, politics, identity).

** Technical requirements for adaption

Each new topic requires:
- 5 HTML source pages (one per type)
- 1 topic hub page linking the 5 sources
- Updates to the landing page grid (index.html)
- New student cards for the topic (5 cards)
- Updated teacher guide source key

The HUD, student card format, group sheet, and classroom activity structure
can all be reused without modification.


* Common Mistakes to Avoid

** Making the bad sources too obvious

If the disinformation source has a header that says "TRUTH WARRIORS" in
flashing red text, students will identify it instantly without evaluating it.
The bad sources should require actual reading to identify. The churnalism
source should look like journalism. The native ad should look like a health
article. The biased blog should feel warm and reasonable, not obviously wrong.

** Making all sources the same length

Real sources of different types are different lengths. A journal article is
long, dense, and structured with clear sections. A personal blog post can be
long and conversational. Churnalism is short — it is designed for fast
consumption. A native advertising piece is medium length, structured to hold
attention until the product placement. Varying length is part of the realism.

** Putting the gold standard first in the source list

If Source 1 is always the best source, students will figure out the pattern
within one or two groups and the exercise loses its challenge. Shuffle the
positions. In this project the gold standard appears at S2, S3, S4, S4, S5
across the five groups.

** Using obviously fake source names

"TrustScienceNow.com" and "WellnessQuacks.net" are not convincing. Real
sources have plausible, professional-sounding names: "Journal of Applied
Microbiology Education", "HealthBuzz Daily NZ", "PureLife Wellness NZ",
"NZ Health Investigates". The names should be plausible enough that a student
could encounter them in the wild without immediately recognising them as fake.

** Forgetting to neutralise visual quality cues

If you code the gold standard source with a green badge and the worst
source with a red badge on the hub page, you have told students the answer
before they start reading. All source cards on the hub page should look
identical — same neutral badge colour, same icon, same text style.

** Skipping the debrief

The debrief is where the learning happens. The jigsaw and the cards are
diagnostic; the debrief is the lesson. Build in 10 minutes minimum. The most
productive debrief question is the third one: "Did any source fool you at
first?" — because honest answers to this question reveal that even careful,
engaged readers can be taken in by native advertising or by sources that
look educational.


* Summary: The Design Principles in One Place

1. Every source must be convincing in its genre.
2. Bad sources should require reading to identify — visual complexity is not
   a tell.
3. Accurate content can still be in an unreliable source (native advertising
   is the best example of this).
4. The gold standard position should be shuffled — never always first.
5. The hub page should be visually neutral — no quality colour-coding.
6. The HUD anchors every page in the classroom context without disrupting
   the reading experience.
7. The student card questions are observation prompts, not a classification
   rubric — students should discover the categories, not apply them.
8. No ties in the group ranking — the difficulty of forced ordering is
   pedagogically productive.
9. Do not reveal source types until the class debrief.
10. The debrief is the lesson. Protect time for it.
