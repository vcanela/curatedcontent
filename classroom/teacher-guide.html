Teacher Guide — Source Audit (Jigsaw)

Year 9 Science · 40 min · 20–25 students · Page 1 of 2

The big picture

  • 5 groups, one topic each. Each student reads one source independently, fills in their card, then shares with the group.
  • Groups rank all 5 sources and answer synthesis questions together.
  • Class debrief surfaces the patterns — you reveal the type names at the end, not before.
Key rule: Do not tell students the sources vary in quality — that is the point of the activity. If asked whether the sources are "real," say: "They're all accessible websites. Your job is to evaluate them."

Timing at a glance

0–3 min Setup Seat groups. Hand out cards — one per student. Write hub URL on board.
3–18 min Solo reading Each student reads their source and fills in their card. No group talk yet.
18–28 min Group ranking Share findings. Rank all 5 sources (no ties). Complete group sheet.
28–38 min Class debrief Each group shares best + worst source. Build a list of tactics on the board.
38–40 min Exit thought Each student writes: "Next time I read something online, I will check…"

What to say at each stage

  • At the start: "Your card tells you which source to open. Read it, answer the five questions — don't discuss yet."
  • Before group ranking: "You must put them in order — no ties. Disagreement is fine."
  • At debrief start: "Tell me your group's top-ranked source. What made it stand out? Now tell me the bottom one — and what made it hard to spot."
  • Reveal moment (end of debrief): Write the five type names on the board as you describe what students found. See Page 2.

With 20 students (4 per group)

Skip Source 5 from each group's ranking, or give one student Sources 4 and 5 to skim together. The ranking works fine with 4 sources.

Where the gold-standard source sits per group

Sources are shuffled — the best source is never Source 1. Students should not be able to guess quality by number.

Debrief — run in this order

1
"What did every group's top-ranked source have in common?"
Build a list: named author, named institution, specific cited evidence, neutral tone. These are your gold-standard signals.
2
"What did most groups' bottom-ranked sources have in common?"
Expect: emotional language, no credentials, vague sourcing. The pattern across different topics is the key insight.
3
"Did any source fool you at first? Which one, and why?"
Target: Semmelweis S5 (looks like a school page) and the native-ad sources (CleanMed, GutWise, TrialConnect, PureLife). Draw out: professional appearance ≠ trustworthy.
4
"Two sources on each topic had real, accurate facts — but they were still not reliable. Why?"
Target: native advertising sources. Point: true facts arranged to serve a commercial purpose is still a form of bias.
5
Reveal: name the types on the board.
Write as you go: Gold Standard → Quality Journalism → Churnalism → Native Advertising → Misinformation → Disinformation. Tell students: "These exist on every topic, not just medicine."
The Semmelweis "trap" source (S5): Looks like a school resource, written with good intent, but contains factual errors — wrong year of death (1868 not 1865), credits Semmelweis with "discovering germ theory," and claims doctors immediately accepted his findings. Best example for discussing currency (last updated 2007). Students who compare it with S4 should notice the discrepancies.
