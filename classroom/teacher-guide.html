Teacher Guide — Source Audit Activity

Year 9 Science · 40 minutes · 20–25 students

What students are doing

Students work in 5 groups (one per topic). Each person in the group reads one source and answers 5 questions on their card. Groups then share findings and produce a ranked list. A whole-class debrief draws out common patterns. No prior framework is introduced — the goal is for students to discover the patterns themselves before you name them.

Lesson timing

TimeWhat's happeningYour role
0 – 3 min Setup. Seat students in their groups. Hand out cards (each student gets one card only). Give groups their ranking sheet. Say: "Your card tells you which source to open. Read it, answer the five questions, and don't discuss it yet — you'll do that in a few minutes." Write the hub URL on the board.
3 – 18 min Individual analysis. Each student reads their one source and fills in their card independently. Circulate quietly. If students ask "what does this mean?" redirect to the card questions. Don't validate or correct their judgements yet — that's the point of the debrief.
18 – 28 min Group ranking. Students share their individual findings with the group. Together they complete the group ranking sheet — ranking all 5 sources 1–5 and answering the three synthesis questions. Remind groups: "You must put them in order — no ties." The disagreement that happens when they can't agree is productive. Let it run.
28 – 38 min Class debrief. Each group shares: their top-ranked source and their bottom-ranked source, plus what made the bottom one hard to spot. See debrief questions on Page 2. Build a list of tactics on the board as groups report. Reveal source type names at the end.
38 – 40 min Exit thought. Each student writes one sentence: "Next time I read something online, I will check…" Collect cards or ask 2–3 students to share aloud.

With 20 students (4 per group)

Assign Sources 1–4 only, skipping Source 5 for each group — or give one student Sources 4 and 5 to cover together (they can skim Source 5 quickly if they finish Source 4 early). The ranking then covers 4 sources, which works fine.

Important: Do not tell students this environment is teacher-designed or that sources vary in quality. The debrief is the reveal moment. If students ask whether the sources are "real," a neutral "They're all accessible websites — your job is to evaluate them" is enough.

Debrief questions — run in this order

1. "What did every group's #1 source have in common?"
Expect: named authors, named institutions, cited evidence, neutral tone. Build a list on the board.
2. "What did most groups' bottom-ranked sources have in common?"
Expect: vague sourcing, emotional language, no credentials. The pattern across different topics is the key insight.
3. "Did any source fool you at first? Which one and why?"
The Semmelweis teacher resource (S5) and the CleanMed NZ / TrialConnect pharma pages are the best candidates here. Draw out: looking professional is not the same as being trustworthy.
4. "Two of the sources on this site had real, accurate facts — but they were still not reliable sources. Why?"
Target: native advertising sources (PureLife, GutWise, CleanMed, TrialConnect). The point: true facts arranged to serve a commercial purpose is still a form of bias.
5. Name the types (reveal).
Write these five terms on the board as you describe what students found: Gold Standard → Quality Journalism → Churnalism → Native Advertising → Misinformation → Disinformation. Tell students: "These types exist on every topic, not just medicine."
The Semmelweis "trap" source (ScienceTeachingResources.net, 2007): This page looks like a school resource and was written with good intent, but it contains several factual errors — including the wrong year of death (1868 instead of 1865), crediting Semmelweis with "discovering germ theory," and claiming doctors immediately accepted his findings. Students who compare it to Source 1 should notice the discrepancies. This is your best example for discussing currency — when was it last updated, and does that matter?
